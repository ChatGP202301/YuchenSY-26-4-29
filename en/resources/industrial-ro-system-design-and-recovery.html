Industrial RO System Design: Recovery, Concentration Factor and Staging

RO recovery is the permeate flow divided by feed flow. Raising recovery reduces concentrate flow but increases concentrate salinity and can increase scaling, concentration polarization and operating risk. Design recovery must be calculated from the complete feed analysis, target flow, membrane model, temperature and staged system limits—not selected from a generic rule.

Mass balance and concentration factor

For a steady-state flow balance, feed = permeate + concentrate. If salt passage is ignored and salts do not precipitate, an idealized concentration factor is approximately 1/(1 − recovery): 75% recovery → 4×, 80% → 5×, 90% → 10×. These are arithmetic illustrations, not design predictions. Real systems require salt passage, precipitation, antiscalant, temperature, staging and recovery limits to be evaluated.

A supplier operating example reports feed 80 m³/h, concentrate 20 m³/h and permeate 60 m³/h, hence 75% recovery. It also estimates recovery from feed/permeate/concentrate TDS. Such a balance is a diagnostic cross-check only when samples are representative, the plant is stable, instruments are calibrated and concentration measurements are comparable. TDS may not track each scaling ion accurately.

Design-case boundary

The supplied technical material’s groundwater design-software case (source pages 33–45) reports 100 m³/h permeate, 25°C and 75% recovery; it also discusses 27 LMH flux, approximately 100 elements and a 2.5 bar interstage boost in an optimization example. The underlying complete water analysis, software input/output file and membrane configuration are unavailable for independent reproduction. Treat those numbers solely as supplier case context, not a recommended design or validated operating point.

Inputs an engineer needs

Full ionic analysis (including hardness, alkalinity, sulfate, silica, barium/strontium, iron and organics where relevant), feed variability, target permeate composition and flow, temperature, pH, pretreatment, recovery, element model, pressure-vessel arrangement, energy constraints, concentrate disposal limits and applicable standards. A single TDS result is insufficient.

Frequently asked questions

Does higher recovery always lower cost?

No. It can reduce concentrate volume while increasing pressure, scaling control, cleaning or reliability costs.

Can the example’s 75% recovery be copied to another site?

No. Feed chemistry and system limits determine allowable recovery.

Can TDS predict scaling?

No. Individual ion concentrations, pH, temperature and concentration in the concentrate are needed.
